The adequacy of a Miranda warning is a question of law. United States v. Connell,

2

869 F.2d 1349, 1351 (9th Cir. 1989). Individuals possess the right to be informed, prior to

3

custodial interrogation, “that [they have] the right to the presence of an attorney, and that

4

if [they] cannot afford an attorney one will be appointed for [them] prior to any questioning

5

if [they] so desire[ ].” Miranda v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 436, 479 (1966). “[W]hat Miranda

6

requires ‘is meaningful advice to the unlettered and unlearned in language which [they]

7

can comprehend and on which [they] can knowingly act.’” Connell, 869 F.2d at 1351

8

(quoting Coyote v. United States, 380 F.2d 305, 308 (10th Cir. 1967)). In order for the

9

warning to be valid, the combination or the wording of its warnings cannot be affirmatively

10

misleading. See Connell, 869 F.2d at 1352. “The warning must be clear and not susceptible

11

to equivocation.” United States v. San Juan-Cruz, 314 F.3d 384, 387 (9th Cir. 2002).

12

“For inculpatory statements made by a defendant during custodial interrogation to

13

be admissible in evidence, the defendant’s waiver of Miranda rights must be voluntary,

14

knowing, and intelligent.” United States v. Garibay, 143 F.3d 534, 536 (9th Cir. 1998).

15

“Only if the totality of the circumstances surrounding the interrogation reveal both an

16

uncoerced choice and the requisite level of comprehension may a court properly conclude

17

that the Miranda rights have been waived.” Moran v. Burdine, 475 U.S. 412, 421 (1986).

18

There is a presumption against waiver which the Government bears the burden of

19

overcoming by a preponderance of evidence. See Garibay, 143 F.3d at 536; see also

20

Berghuis v. Thompkins, 560 U.S. 370, 384 (2012) (“[T]his ‘heavy burden’ is not more than

21

the burden to establish waiver by a preponderance of the evidence.”). The validity of a

22

waiver depends upon the particular facts and circumstances of the case, including the

23

background, experience and conduct of the defendant. See Edwards v. Arizona, 451 U.S.

24

477, 482 (1981). “Several factors to consider are (i) defendant’s mental capacity; (ii)

25

whether the defendant signed a written waiver; (iii) whether the defendant was advised in

26

his native tongue or had a translator; (iv) whether the defendant appeared to understand his

27

rights; (v) whether the defendant’s rights were individually and repeatedly explained to

28

4

investigation . . . about your application for admission to the United States to-day. So, the

5

government is charging you with 8 U.S.C. 1324 which is alien smuggling. Before I ask you

6

any questions, I need to read you your rights and I need to make sure you understand your

7

rights, ok? I’m gonna read one by one. You have the right to remain silent. Do you

8

understand?” Defendant responded “Mm-hmm.” The officer stated “Anything you say

9

can be used against you in court or in any immigration or administrative proceeding.”

10

Defendant responded “Mm-hmm.” (ECF No. 42-1 at 7-8). The officer clearly informed

11

the Defendant that the “government is charging you with 8 U.S.C. 1324 which is alien

12

smuggling” and that “Anything you say can be used against you in court.” The warnings

13

conveyed clearly that Defendant was being charged with a criminal offense and that his

14

statements could be used against him in court. These warnings were “clear and not

15

susceptible to equivocation.” San Juan-Cruz, 314 F.3d at 387.

16

The officer informed Defendant of his “right to talk to a lawyer for advice before we

17

ask you any questions and to have him with you during questioning” and “If you decide to

18

answer questions now, without a lawyer present, you will still have the right to stop

19

answering at any time.” Defendant indicated that he understood and the officer continued,

20

“And you also have the right to stop answering at any time, until you talk to a lawyer.”

21

(ECF No. 42-1). The officer clearly informed Defendant that he had a right to talk to a

22

lawyer before any questioning and that he had the right to stop answering any time and talk

23

24

indicate any intention to terminate the interview or to seek the advice of counsel.
