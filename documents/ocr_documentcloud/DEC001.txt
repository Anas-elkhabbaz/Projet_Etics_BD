II.

Legal Standard

4

a. The Booking Exception

5

In the seminal case of Miranda, the Supreme Court created a prophylactic

6

protection to the Fifth Amendment: “the prosecution may not use statements, whether

7

exculpatory or inculpatory, stemming from custodial interrogation of the defendant

8

unless it demonstrates the use of procedural safeguards effective to secure the privilege

9

against self-incrimination.” Miranda v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 436, 444 (1966).

10

Thirty-six years later, the Supreme Court exempted from Miranda’s protection

11

responses to questions intended “to secure the biographical data necessary to complete

12

booking or pretrial services.” Pennsylvania v. Muniz, 496 U.S. 582, 601 (1990) (plurality

13

opinion) (quotation marks omitted) (concluding that the answers to questions regarding

14

the defendant’s name, address, height, weight, eye color, date of birth, and current age

15

were admissible in the absence of Miranda warnings). Because such questions “rarely

16

elicit an incriminating response, routine gathering of biographical data does not constitute

17

interrogation sufficient to trigger constitutional protections,” United States v. Gonzalez–

18

Sandoval, 894 F.2d 1043, 1046 (9th Cir. 1990), even if asked after a defendant has

19

invoked the right to counsel. See United States v. Zapien, 861 F.3d 971, 975 (9th Cir.

20

2017) (per curiam).

21

The exception does not apply, however, to questioning “conducted for an

22

investigatory purpose and not for the booking process,” United States v. Poole, 794 F.2d

23

462, 466 (9th Cir. 1986) (as amended), or questioning that is “reasonably likely to elicit

24

an incriminating response from the suspect,” United States v. Booth, 669 F.2d 1231, 1237

25

(9th Cir. 1981) (quotation omitted). To account for the “potential for abuse by law

26

enforcement officers who might, under the guise of seeking ‘objective’ or ‘neutral’

27
28

5
19-CR-0838-GPC

 Case 3:19-cr-00838-GPC Document 37 Filed 12/18/19 PageID.293 Page 6 of 13

1

information, deliberately elicit an incriminating statement from a suspect,” id., the Court

2

has adopted an “objective” test. Zapien, 861 F.3d at 975 (citing United States v.

3

Washington, 462 F.3d 1124, 1132 (9th Cir. 2006)). Under this test, “context” is key.

4

Zapien, 861 F.3d at 975 (citing United States v. Pacheco-Lopez, 531 F.3d 420, 424–25

5

(6th Cir. 2008)). After all, “[e]ven a relatively innocuous question may, in light of the

6

unusual susceptibility of a particular suspect, be reasonably likely to elicit an

7

incriminating response.” Booth, 669 F.2d at 1238.

8

In particular, the “relationship of the question asked to the crime suspected is

9

highly relevant.” United States v. Mata-Abundiz, 717 F.2d 1277, 1280 (9th Cir. 1983).

10

For example, ‘[w]hile there is usually nothing objectionable about asking a detainee his

11

place of birth, the same question assumes a completely different character when an INS

12

agent asks it of a person he suspects is an illegal alien.” United States v. Henley, 984 F.2d

13

1040, 1042 (9th Cir. 1993). Likewise, “when a defendant [is] charged with murder,” their

14

“unadmonished responses to questions about [a] gang affiliation” are protected by

15

Miranda. United States v. Williams, 842 F.3d 1143, 1148 (9th Cir. 2016).

16

For a question to fall within the ambit of Miranda, the likeliness to produce an

17

incriminating response should be objectively apparent when the question is asked. United

18

States v. Salgado, 292 F.3d 1169, 1174 (9th Cir. 2002) (considering, as one factor, that

19

defendant’s “response became incriminating only after the passage of a substantial period

20

of time”). Also, the “subjective intent of the agent is relevant but not conclusive.” United

21

States v. Mata-Abundiz, 717 F.2d 1277, 1280 (9th Cir. 1983). And, that an officer already

22

knows the answer to the question asked does not necessarily show that officer seeks to

23

elicit an incriminating response. United States v. Washington, 462 F.3d 1124, 1133 n.1

24

(9th Cir. 2006) (“A desire to confirm the information for booking purposes does not

25

mean that the question constitutes interrogation.”)

a. The Pre-Miranda Questioning Falls Within the Booking Exception.

3

Defendant asks that the Court suppress all statements made during the pre-Miranda

4

questioning on the basis that Agent Mendoza’s questions “were far beyond the limited

5

6

7

8
9

As a threshold matter, the Court notes that the Government’s reliance on ICE Form
73 is misplaced. (Cf. ECF No. 31 at 4–5.) Every court has a duty to examine each case

10

under the booking questions exception in light of the specific circumstances and

11

“context” attendant to it. Pennsylvania, 496 U.S. at 601–02; Zapien, 861 F.3d at 976

12

(considering both “the questions themselves and the context in which they were asked “).

13

Thus, an agent’s use of a form, even if standard and widely adopted, affords no

14

talismanic protection to the questions they ask. See United States v. Mateo, 392 F. Supp.

15

3d 454, 466 (D. Vt. 2019) (rejecting questions from a U.S. Marshall form regarding

16

“Defendant's siblings’ names, addresses, dates of birth, and phone numbers” and

17

“whether Defendant used drugs and drank alcohol” as likely “to elicit incriminating

18

responses and . . . not needed for any administrative purpose”); see also Rosa v. McCray,

19

396 F.3d 210, 222 (2d Cir. 2005) (asking whether certain questions were “narrowly

20

crafted by the officer to obtain information necessary to complete the booking form”).

21

This is evident even here, with respect to ICE Form 73, as the Government

b. The Defendant’s Statements Were Voluntary.

2

While the Court provided an oral ruling on October 4, 2019 that the Defendant’s

3

statements to interviewing officers were voluntary, the Court herein supplements its

4

earlier decision with the following discussion and analysis.

5

Defendant argues that the Agents’ conduct and the circumstances in totality

6

functioned to overbear Defendant’s will. (ECF No. 20 at 5.) Defendant points to the facts

7

that: there were two officers; Defendant was under arrest; Defendant lacked counsel;

8

Defendant’s invocation was ignored; and the Agent “represent[ed] that he could

9

favorably influence the outcome of the case by writing to the judge and speaking to the

10
11

prosecutor” if Defendant told the “truth.” (ECF No. 20 at 5.)
In response, the Government asserts the contrary: “[a]ll of the evidence supports a

12

finding a voluntariness.” (ECF No. 31 at 8.) The Government posits that Defendant did

13

not appear sick, was not threatened, did not appear under the influence of narcotics or

14

alcohol, did not appear low functioning, did not ask to stop the interview or complain

15

about the interview’s conditions, and was not handcuffed. (ECF No. 31 at 8–9; ECF No.

16

29, Aug. Hrg. at 12, 36, 37.) The Government also notes that the Agents did not raise

17

their voices during the interview, were not armed, and wore plain clothes. (ECF No. 31 at

18

9; ECF No. 29, Aug. Hrg. at 12, 37.) The Government further asserts that neither agent

19

pressured Defendant, both agents were calm, the interview lasted approximately 35

20

minutes in total, and Defendant both orally, and by completing the waiver form,

21

knowingly waived Miranda. (ECF No. 21 at 10, 12–13; ECF No. 20-1, Ex. B.)

22

“A confession is involuntary if coerced either by physical intimidation or

23

psychological pressure.” United States v. Haswood, 350 F.3d 1024, 1027 (9th Cir. 2003).

24

The Court examines this by asking “whether the defendant’s will was overborne at the

25

time he confessed.” United States v. Crawford, 372 F.3d 1048, 1060 (9th Cir. 2004) (en

26
27
28

11

1

banc). The government bears the burden of proving that a confession is voluntary by a

2

preponderance of the evidence. Colorado v. Connelly, 479 U.S. 157, 168 (1986).

3

“Voluntariness depends on such factors as the surrounding circumstances and the

4

combined effect of the entire course of the officers’ conduct upon the defendant.” Henry

5

v. Kernan, 197 F.3d 1021, 1026 (9th Cir. 1999).
Here, the Court cannot say that Defendant’s statements were involuntary. The

6
7

Agents “advised [Defendant] of his rights both verbally and in writing,” United States v.

8

Lacey, 225 F. App’x 478, 480 (9th Cir. 2007), “and were polite and respectful.” United

9

States v. Barboza, 399 F. App’x 312, 313 (9th Cir. 2010). Agent Mendoza’s offer to help

10

the Defendant also “does not render a subsequent statement involuntary,” United States v.

11

Leon Guerrero, 847 F.2d 1363, 1366 (9th Cir. 1988) (citations omitted), nor did his

12

references to Defendant’s family rise to the level of a Tingle violation. See United States

13

v. Tingle, 658 F.2d 1332, 1334 (9th Cir. 1981) (finding confession involuntary where

14

“[i]n an effort to obtain a confession, [the officer] told [the defendant] either that she

15

16

In sum, while Defendant is correct that two officers wearing visible badges

17

interrogated him, Defendant’s condition is indistinguishable from most people taken to

18

secondary inspection at the Port of Entry. (ECF No. 29, Aug. Hrg. at 42.) Consequently,

19

because Defendant’s declaration was stricken, (ECF No. 20-1, Ex. C; ECF No. 29, Aug.

20

Hrg. at 59), and Defendant introduced no other witnesses or exhibits, there is no evidence

21

to contradict the prosecution’s assertion of voluntariness except the fact that the Agents

22

engaged in the booking questions after Defendant’s invocation. On these facts, the Court

23

finds that the prosecution has proven Defendants’ statements were voluntary.

that it does not intend to offer these statements in it case-in-chief. As such, the motion to

7

suppress these statements is DENIED as MOOT.

10

12
