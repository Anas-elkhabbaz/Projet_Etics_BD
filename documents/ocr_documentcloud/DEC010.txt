BACKGROUND

2

On May 25, 2017, at approximately 2:00 p.m., Defendant approached the pre-

3

primary inspection area of the Highway 86 Border Patrol checkpoint near Westmorland,

4

California. Border Patrol Agent Larry Orozco and his certified canine partner ‘Louisza’

5

were patrolling the pre-primary area. Defendant testified during the evidentiary hearing

6

that he observed the agent and his canine walking around the passenger side and the of

7

Defendant’s vehicle. Defendant testified that Agent Orozco and Louisza did not walk

8

around the driver’s side of the vehicle. According to Agent Orozco, as Defendant

9

approached the primary inspection booth, he and Louisza walked around to the driver’s

10

side of Defendant’s vehicle, where Louisza “alerted to the bottom of the driver’s side

11

door seam.” Doc. No. 32 at 26.1 Agent Orozco relayed the canine alert to the primary

12

inspection officer who referred Defendant to secondary inspection. In secondary

13

inspection, Defendant consented to the search of his vehicle. Louisza again alerted to the

14

driver’s side door area. Agent Orozco removed the driver’s side rocker panel on the

15

vehicle, and removed six packages that tested positive for heroin.

16

Defendant was advised of his Miranda rights, signed a written waiver of his

17

Miranda rights, and admitted to knowingly smuggling the narcotics. Defendant also

18

signed a written consent form to search his cell phone, which uncovered additional

19

incriminating evidence.

1.

Highway 86 Checkpoint
Defendant argues that the government is using the Highway 86 checkpoint for the

7

prohibited purpose of drug interdiction. Defendant challenges the constitutionality of the

8

checkpoint, arguing that the government is using it for general law enforcement purposes,

9

instead of immigration objectives.

10

The Fourth Amendment requires that searches and seizures be reasonable. See U.S.

11

CONST. amend. IV. Generally, a search or seizure is unreasonable in the absence of

12

individualized suspicion of wrongdoing. Id. While a vehicle stop at an immigration

13

checkpoint is a “seizure,” Border Patrol may stop or slow vehicles, and question

14

occupants at a checkpoint for Fourth Amendment purposes. See United States v.

15

Martinez-Fuente, 428 U.S. 543, 562 (1976). Permanent interior checkpoints, such as the

16

Highway 86 checkpoint, are maintained at or near important roads leading away from the

17

border and “operate on a coordinated basis designed to avoid circumvention by

18

smugglers and others who transport the illegal aliens.” Id. at 552. A vehicle may be

19

stopped at a permanent immigration checkpoint for brief initial questioning, and then

20

referred to a secondary inspection area for further questioning “in the absence of an

21

individualized suspicion.” Id. at 562. An officer has wide discretion in making referrals

22

to secondary. See id. at 564.

23

Here, the evidence supports a finding that Border Patrol is permissibly operating

24

the Highway 86 checkpoint for the primary purpose of immigration control. See, e.g.,

25

Doc. No. 19-9 at 2. Presented with similar statistics and a similar record of the Border

26

Patrol’s operation of the checkpoint, other judges in this District have determined that the

27

Highway 86 checkpoint is constitutional. See, e.g., United States v. Summers, 153 F.

28

Supp. 3d 1261, 1266-67 (S.D. Cal. 2015) (“During the past three year period, there were
3

approximately 1,746 apprehensions at the checkpoint, including 1,579 immigration-

Likewise, in this case it appears that the checkpoint was being operated for the

4

5

9

10

2. Vehicle Search

12

Defendant argues that even if the Highway 86 checkpoint is legal, agents lacked

13

14

15

16

21

warrantless search of a vehicle at secondary inspection if, in the totality of the
