III. DISCUSSION

13

Defendant moves to suppress any and all statements she made during

14

Special Agents Cheviron and Fenoglio’s interview on May 22, 2018, arguing that

15

her statements were made without the advisal of rights mandated by Miranda v.

16

Arizona, 384 U.S. 436 (1966) and were involuntary. The Government opposes

17

suppression, arguing that Defendant's statements were voluntary and that

18

Miranda’s warnings were not required because Defendant's statements were not

19

the product of custodial interrogation.

20

Under Miranda, advisal and waiver of certain Fifth and Sixth Amendment

21

rights must precede custodial interrogation. Defendant was at her home at the

22

time of her questioning. When a “suspect has not formally been taken into police

23

custody, a suspect is nevertheless considered ‘in custody’ for purposes of

24

Miranda if the suspect has been ‘deprived of his freedom of action in any

25

significant way.’” United States v. Craighead, 539 F.3d 1073, 1082 (9th Cir.

26

2008) (citing Miranda, 384 U.S. at 444). “To determine whether the suspect was

27

in custody,” courts “first examine the totality of the circumstances surrounding the

28

interrogation.” Id. Courts “then ask whether a reasonable person in those
3
19-cr-03255-BTM

 Case 3:19-cr-03255-BTM Document 340 Filed 11/18/20 PageID.1236 Page 4 of 10

1

circumstances would have felt he or she was not at liberty to terminate the

2

interrogation and leave.” Id.

3

“The element of compulsion that concerned the Court in Miranda is less

4

likely to be present where the suspect is in familiar surroundings. Nevertheless,

5

an interrogation in the suspect's home may be found to be custodial under

6

certain circumstances.” Id. at 1083. In determining whether an in-home

7

interrogation is custodial, the Ninth Circuit “considers the extent to which the

8

circumstances of the interrogation turned the otherwise comfortable and familiar

9

surroundings of the home into a ‘police-dominated atmosphere.’” Id. This

10

determination “is necessarily fact intensive.” Id. at 1084. The Ninth Circuit has

11

identified several relevant factors for courts to consider: (1) the number of law

12

enforcement personnel and whether they were armed; (2) whether the suspect

13

was at any point restrained, either by physical force or by threats; (3) whether the

14

suspect was isolated from others; and (4) whether the suspect was informed that

15

he was free to leave or terminate the interview, and the context in which any

16

such statements were made. Id.

17

First, the large number of law enforcement officers that entered

18

Defendant’s home, approximately half of which were armed, and which consisted

19

of multiple agencies, weighs in favor of a finding that Defendant’s home was

20

turned into a police-dominated atmosphere. As the Ninth Circuit in Craighead

21

noted:

22
23
24
25
26
27
28

When a large number of law enforcement personnel enter a suspect's
home, they may fill the home such that there are no police-free rooms
or spaces to which the suspect may retreat should he wish to
terminate the interrogation. Similarly, when the number of law
enforcement personnel far outnumber the suspect, the suspect may
reasonably believe that, should he attempt to leave, he will be
stopped by one of the many officers he will encounter on the way out.
The suspect may also believe that the large number of officers was
brought for the purpose of preventing his departure. In addition, if the
suspect sees the officers unholstering their weapons within his home,
4

1

4

the suspect may reasonably believe that his home is no longer safe
from the threat of police force. In short, the presence of a large
number of visibly armed law enforcement officers goes a long way
towards making the suspect's home a police-dominated atmosphere.
Id. at 1084–85.

5

Here, ten law enforcement officers from three different identifiable agencies

6

entered Defendant’s approximately 1500 square foot one story home in the early

7

morning. Five of those law enforcement officers were armed agents, who

8

conducted an initial safety sweep wearing ballistic vests and with their weapons

9

unholstered. The number of law enforcement officers clearly outnumbered

10

Defendant, her two minor children, and the other adult female present in the

11

Residence. Further, the number of law enforcement officers in the comparatively

12

small home resulted in few to no spaces that were free of law enforcement

13

presence. In addition, as the Ninth Circuit noted, the participation of three

14

different law enforcement agencies, with officers wearing identifiable agency

15

insignias, would likely have caused confusion about each agency’s coordination

16

and authority to let Defendant go free. See id. at 1085 (the presence of three

17

different law enforcement agencies created confusion in defendant as to whether

18

the remaining agencies might prevent him from leaving, even if one agency told

19

him he was free to go). As such, a reasonable person in Defendant’s position

20

would feel that her home was dominated by a police presence. See United

21

States v. Clymer, 524 F. App'x 354, 355 (9th Cir. 2013) (finding an in-home

22

interrogation to be custodial where “eleven federal and local law enforcement

23

officers, representing six different agencies, executed a search warrant at

24

[defendant’s] residence [and] [u]pon entering the residence, the officers drew

25

their weapons”); United States v. McKany, 649 F. App'x 553, 554-55 (9th Cir.

26

2016) (finding a police-dominated atmosphere where “[o]fficers swarmed into

27

[defendant’s] home at 6:30 a.m. in full tactical gear and with guns drawn” and

28

“eight to ten officers initially entered the house, and fourteen officers were

4

United States v. Remy, 393 F. App'x 427, 429 (9th Cir. 2010) (finding that

5

16

In particular, the Court highlights that a mere 10 to 15 minutes elapsed

17

between the time when five armed law enforcement agents entered Defendant’s

18

home, wearing ballistic vests, and with their weapons unholstered, and when

19

Defendant was interviewed. The police-dominated atmosphere created by the

20

armed entry of law enforcement into Defendant’s home could not have dissipated

21

within such a short time frame.

22

Second, the record does not indicate that Defendant was restrained at any

23

point, either by physical force or by threats. Therefore, this factor weighs against

24

a finding of a police-dominated atmosphere.

25

Third, Defendant’s separation during her questioning from the other

26

occupants of the Residence, particularly from her 5-year-old and 14-year-old

27

children, weigh in favor of a finding of a police-dominated atmosphere. “A

28

frequently recurring example of police domination concerns the removal of the
6

1

suspect from the presence of family, friends, or colleagues who might lend moral

2

support during the questioning and deter a suspect from making inculpatory

3

statements.” Craighead, 539 F.3d at 1087. “[T]he Supreme Court [has been]

4

explicit that the law enforcement technique of isolating the suspect from family

5

and friends is one of the distinguishing features of a custodial interrogation.” Id.

6

Here, Defendant was questioned in the back patio of her house, at the

7

direction of the questioning FBI agents. During the questioning, her two minor

8

children remained in the living room with at least one law enforcement officer.

9

While Defendant’s back patio does not create the same level of physical isolation

10

as the storage room interrogation in Craighead, the isolation of Defendant from

11

her two minor children, particularly in the context of multiple armed law

12

enforcement agents entering her home a mere 10 to 15 minutes earlier, would

13

lead a reasonable person in Defendant’s position to feel cut off from her moral

14

support or be preoccupied with her children’s wellbeing. As such, this factor

15

weighs in favor of a finding of a police-dominated atmosphere. See Remy, 393

16

F. App'x at 429 (finding that defendant was not in custody at home where

17

defendant was in “telephonic contact with his wife during the interview”); Holley,

18

526 F. App'x 743, 745 (9th Cir. 2013) (finding no police-dominated atmosphere

19

where defendant “was not isolated from the members of his family, who were in

20

the room when he was questioned”); United States v. Busby, 613 F. App'x 627,

21

630 (9th Cir. 2015) (finding no custodial interrogation where two officers

22

“questioned [defendant] at his house, [and] permitted his wife to remain for the

23

questioning”); Wolf, 472 F. App'x at 550 (9th Cir. 2012) (finding no custodial

24

interrogation where “[t]he officers permitted [defendant] to keep her baby with her

25

during their questioning”).

26

Fourth, Defendant was informed that the questioning was voluntary and

27

that she was free to leave or terminate the interview. However, given the

28

circumstances in which Defendant was given these statements, these statements
7

1

did not render her interrogation non-custodial. “The mere recitation of the

2

statement that the suspect is free to leave or terminate the interview, however,

3

does not render an interrogation non-custodial per se. [Courts] must consider

4

the delivery of these statements within the context of the scene as a whole.”

5

Craighead, 539 F.3d at 1088. “The Miranda test for custody does not ask

6

whether the suspect was told that he was free to leave; the test asks whether a

7

reasonable person would have felt he or she was not at liberty to terminate the

8

interrogation and leave.” Id. (internal citation and quotation omitted).

9

Here, (1) ten law enforcement officers entered Defendant’s home, (2) early

10

in the morning at a time a person reasonably would not be prepared to leave

11

their home, (3) five of the officers were armed, and entered Defendant’s home

12

wearing ballistic vests and weapons unholstered, (4) the officers represented

13

three different and identifiable law enforcement agencies, (5) Defendant was

14

interviewed only 10 to 15 minutes after the armed home entry by law

15

enforcement, (6) Defendant was isolated from her two minor children during the

16

interview, and (7) the number of law enforcement officers conducting the search

17

in Defendant’s one story, approximately 1500 square foot residence left

18

essentially no space free from their presence. These factors severely limit where

19

Defendant reasonably could have gone. As the Ninth Circuit observed:

20
21
22
23
24
25
26
27
28

If a reasonable person is interrogated inside his own home and is told
he is “free to leave,” where will he go? The library? The police
station? He is already in the most constitutionally protected place on
earth. To be “free” to leave is a hollow right if the one place the
suspect cannot go is his own home. Similarly, a reasonable person
interrogated inside his own home may have a different understanding
of whether he is truly free to terminate the interrogation if his home is
crawling with law enforcement agents conducting a warrant-approved
search. He may not feel that he can successfully terminate the
interrogation if he knows that he cannot empty his home of his
interrogators until they have completed their search.
Craighead, 539 F. 3d at 1083. See also McKany, 649 F. App'x at 555 (“a

reasonable person in [defendant’s] position would not have felt free to leave”

2

where “officer's advisal that [defendant] was free to leave occurred in the context

3

of a fourteen-agent search”).

4

Considering the totality of the circumstances under Craighead’s four

5

factors, the Court finds that Defendant’s home had become a police-dominated

6

atmosphere. Although Defendant was not restrained by physical force or threats,

7

and was told that she was free to leave, a reasonable person in her same

8

position would not believe there was anywhere else to go at 6:15 a.m. in the

9

morning, separated from her two minor children, with ten law enforcement

10

officers from three different agencies occupying her small home, 10 to 15

11

minutes after five of the officers entered her home armed, wearing ballistic vests,

12

and with their weapons unholstered. The interrogation of Defendant was

13

custodial, and Miranda warnings were required.

14

While the Court concludes that on May 22, 2018, Defendant was subjected

15

to custodial interrogation without being provided the advisal of rights required by

16

Miranda, the Court finds that Defendant’s statements were voluntary in nature.

17

FBI Special Agents Cheviron and Fenoglio identified themselves to Defendant as

18

federal law enforcement agents and informed her that she was not under arrest.

19

The agents asked Defendant if she would be willing to speak to them and answer

20

questions, to which she agreed. During the course of the interview, the agents

21

did not confront Defendant with evidence of guilt, did not promise leniency if

22

Defendant answered questions, did not threaten or coerce Defendant, did not

23

gesture to or unholster their firearms, and did not raise their voices. Further,

24

according to Agent Cheviron’s observation of Defendant during the interview,

25

Defendant did not appear to be under the influence of alcohol, drugs, or other

26

medication, did not appear ill, appeared to be fluent in English, and did not seem

27

uncomfortable, confused, or unable to understand the questions asked.

28

Having found that Defendant’s May 22, 2018 statements were voluntary

2

3

Harris v. New York, 401 U.S. 222, 224 (1971) (“It does not follow from Miranda

4

that evidence inadmissible against an accused in the prosecution's case in chief

5

6

10

12

III. CONCLUSION
Based upon the foregoing, the Defendant’s motion to suppress her May 22,

15

2018 statements is GRANTED IN PART and DENIED IN PART.

Because

16

Defendant’s May 22, 2018 statements were made during custodial interrogation

17

but without the warnings mandated by Miranda, they may not be introduced at trial

18

during the Government’s case-in-chief. Because such statements were otherwise

19

voluntary, however, they may be used by the Government where appropriate to

20

impeach the Defendant.
