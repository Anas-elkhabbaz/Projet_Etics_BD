Challenges to Streamline Process

19

Defendant argues that the Government’s use of the “Streamline” process to

20

21

22

23

24

25

At their initial appearance, they have the opportunity to meet with an attorney who can

26

help them negotiate a disposition. CVB defendants are also typically able to avoid any

27

appearance through payment of a fine. Defendant argues that the Government

28

3

Because they are punishable by no more than six months, § 1325 offenses are

4

5

Selective Prosecution and Selective Enforcement

27

Defendant argues that his prosecution under the Streamline process violates the

28

prohibition on selective prosecution and selective enforcement. “A selective-prosecution
7
3:20-mj-20066-AHG

 Case 3:20-mj-20066-AHG Document 35 Filed 08/05/20 PageID.229 Page 8 of 16

1

claim is not a defense on the merits to the criminal charge itself, but an independent

2

assertion that the prosecutor has brought the charge for reasons forbidden by the

3

Constitution.” United States v. Armstrong, 517 U.S. 456, 463 (1996). The standard for

4

proving a claim of selective prosecution is demanding. Id. The executive branch, through

5

the Attorney General and United States Attorneys, has wide discretion in enforcing

6

criminal laws. Id. at 464. As long as there is probable cause to support a prosecution,

7

“whether or not to prosecute, and what charge to file or bring before a grand jury,” falls

8

squarely within the prosecutor’s discretion. Id.; see also United States v. Batchelder, 442

9

U.S. 114, 124 (1979) (“Whether to prosecute and what charge to file or bring before a

10

grand jury are decisions that generally rest in the prosecutor’s discretion.”).

11

Defendant does not argue that the fact he was charged under § 1325 reflects selective

12

prosecution. Instead, he argues that the manner in which his charge was processed reflects

13

selective prosecution. It is not clear that the manner of processing, rather than the fact of

14

prosecution, can be the basis of a claim for selective prosecution. Nonetheless, Defendant

15

has not produced any evidence that the § 1325 prosecutions of any similarly situated

16

individuals were processed using any method other than Streamline. CVB defendants and

17

§ 1325 defendants are not similarly situated merely because their offenses carry

18

comparable punishments. As discussed above, alienage is an element of § 1325, and the

19

statute passes constitutional muster due to Congress’s “broad power over immigration and

20

naturalization.” Fiallo, 430 U.S. at 792.

21

Defendant’s claim that the charge should be dismissed because of selective

22

enforcement also fails. A selective enforcement claim focuses on the investigatory

23

decisions of government agencies, such as the United States Customs and Border Patrol.

24

United States v. Sellers, 906 F.3d 848, 853 (9th Cir. 2018). Although investigatory

25

decisions of agencies are not entitled to the same deference as prosecutorial decisions by

26

the Attorney General or United States Attorneys, id., Defendant’s selective enforcement

27

claim relates solely to how the charge brought against him by the United States Attorney’s

28

office was processed. Defendant’s claim is not related to any investigatory activity by the
8
3:20-mj-20066-AHG

 Case 3:20-mj-20066-AHG Document 35 Filed 08/05/20 PageID.230 Page 9 of 16

1

Border Patrol or any other federal agency preceding the charge. The Government’s use of

2

the Streamline process does not amount to improper selective prosecution or enforcement.

3

Therefore, the Court denies Defendant’s motion to dismiss on the grounds that

4

Defendant’s prosecution violates the principles of selective prosecution and selective

5

enforcement.

Violation of Procedural and Substantive Due Process

7

Defendant argues that his prosecution violates substantive due process because it

8

shocks the conscience to deprive § 1325 defendants of the benefits of the CVB process.

9

The Court disagrees. Defendant’s arrest and detention were supported by a showing of

10

probable cause, which was reviewed and approved by a magistrate judge. Following his

11

detention, Defendant was brought to the federal building, where he met with an attorney.

12

A magistrate judge appointed counsel to represent him and granted his motion for

13

reconsideration of bond. None of these procedures shocks the conscience.

14

Defendant’s claim that his right to procedural due process was violated also fails. A

15

procedural due process analysis considers three factors: 1) the “private interest that will be

16

affected by the official action;” 2) “the risk of an erroneous deprivation of such interest

17

through the procedures used, and the probable value, if any, of additional or substitute

18

procedural safeguards;” and 3) “the Government’s interest, including the function involved

19

and the fiscal and administrative burdens that the additional or substitute procedural

20

requirement would entail.” Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S. 319, 335 (1976).

21

Without more specific information concerning Defendant’s arrest and detention, the

22

Court cannot take at face value Defendant’s contention that the individual interest at stake

23

is “substantial” due to the “traumatizing experience of being held in custody in substandard

24

conditions, and an overwhelmingly greater chance of being convicted of an offense.” ECF

25

No. 20-1 at 16. To be sure, the liberty interest that is implicated when an individual is

26

detained by the Government is substantial. But that interest must be weighed against the

27

Government’s interest, and as discussed above, the Government has put forth legitimate

28

reasons why processing § 1325 defendants through the substitute process of the CVB
9
3:20-mj-20066-AHG

1

would not be practicable. Further, the procedures that have been put into place through the

2

3

interest. Those procedures include a probable cause review to support detention,

4

appointment of counsel, and consideration of pretrial release conditions under the Bail

5

Violation of the Non-Delegation Doctrine

10

Defendant argues that the § 1325 charge against him should be dismissed under the

11

non-delegation doctrine because Congress delegated its core legislative functions to the

12

Executive Branch by allowing “immigration officers” to determine the times and places

13

when an alien can lawfully enter the United States. See 8 U.S.C. § 1325(a) (defining as one

14

of three alternative elements of the offense the entry or attempted entry into the United

15

States “at any time or place other than as designated by immigration officers”). The non-

16

delegation doctrine generally forbids Congress from transferring “powers which are strictly

17

and exclusively legislative” to other branches of government. Gundy v. United States, 139

18

S. Ct. 2116, 2123 (2019) (citation omitted). Defendant claims that immigration officials

19

have unfettered discretion to designate times and places for entry on an ad hoc basis, and

20

that the statute does not provide any intelligible principle to guide this discretion.

21

“In our increasingly complex society, replete with ever changing and more technical

22

problems, [the Supreme] Court has understood that Congress simply cannot do its job

23

absent an ability to delegate power under broad general directives.” Gundy, 139 S. Ct. at

24

2123 (quoting Mistretta v. United States, 488 U.S. 361, 372 (1989)) (internal alteration and

25

quotations omitted). Therefore, as long as “Congress has supplied an intelligible principle

26

to guide the delegee’s use of discretion[,]” a statute does not run afoul of the non-delegation

27

doctrine. In performing the analysis of whether such an “intelligible principle” is supplied,

28

the Court must “constru[e] the challenged statute to figure out what task it delegates and
10
3:20-mj-20066-AHG

 Case 3:20-mj-20066-AHG Document 35 Filed 08/05/20 PageID.232 Page 11 of 16

1

what instructions it provides.” Gundy, 139 S. Ct. at 2123. Importantly, that task of

2

construction does not require the Court to look only at the text of the statute “in isolation.”

3

Rather, the intelligibility of the standards that guide the delegee’s discretion must be

4

considered in light of “the purpose of the [statute at issue], its factual background, and the

5

statutory context in which they appear.” Am. Power & Light Co. v. SEC, 329 U.S. 90, 104

6

(1946).

7

Construing § 1325 in United States v. Nunez-Soberanis, 406 F. Supp. 3d 835 (S.D.

8

Cal. 2019) (Dembin, J.), another court in this District rejected the same non-delegation

9

argument Defendant presents here, reasoning as follows:

10

27

Defendant attempts to read into the statute a broader delegation than
actually occurred by arguing that any individual immigration official can
designate any piece of land as a place for entry. Not so. Congress requires
that aliens seeking lawful entrance to the United States do so at a port of
entry. See United States v. Corrales-Vasquez, 931 F.3d 944, 946 (9th Cir.
2019); United States v. Aldana, 878 F.3d 877, 882 (9th Cir. 2017). Ports
of entry can only be designated or de-designated by the Secretary of
Homeland Security subject to the Administrative Procedures Act. See 8
CFR § 100.4(a). Ports of entry also necessarily include facilities, staffed
by immigration officials that are set up to accept applications for
admission. Aldana, 878 F.3d at 882. To interpret Section 1325(a) to permit
a border patrol agent to designate a portion of the border fence “on a whim”
is in direct conflict with Congress’s clear statutory scheme.
Id. at 839-40. This Court agrees. Contrary to Defendant’s argument, when viewed in the
proper context rather than viewing the statutory text in isolation, § 1325’s delegation to
immigration officers to designate time and place of lawful entry is not unfettered, but
significantly constrained. Am. Power, 329 U.S. at 104; see also Gundy, 139 S. Ct. at 212324 (considering the text of a statute “alongside its context, purpose, and history” to
determine the extent of discretion afforded to the delegee in question).
Congress’s delegation of the duty to designate the time and place for lawful entry of
aliens also does not expand the scope of criminal liability under § 1325. Opening or closing
ports of entry does not change or expand the conduct that leads to criminal liability under

28
11
3:20-mj-20066-AHG

 Case 3:20-mj-20066-AHG Document 35 Filed 08/05/20 PageID.233 Page 12 of 16

1

§ 1325. Closing ports or restricting hours of operation may make it more cumbersome for

2

aliens to enter legally, but it does not create new or different criminal liability.

3
4

Therefore, the Court denies Defendant’s motion to dismiss on the grounds that
§ 1325 violates the non-delegation doctrine.

Vagueness

6

Defendant argues the charge against him should be dismissed because

7

8 U.S.C. § 1325 is void for vagueness. A statute can be impermissibly vague in either of

8

two circumstances: 1) the statute “fails to provide people of ordinary intelligence a

9

reasonable opportunity to understand what conduct it prohibits;” or 2) the statute

10

“authorizes or even encourages arbitrary and discriminatory enforcement.” Hill v.

11

Colorado, 530 U.S. 703, 732 (2000). Defendant argues that § 1325 is impermissibly vague

12

because it permits immigration officers unlimited discretion to decide what places to

13

designate for lawful entry. As discussed above, this is not correct. Individual Border Patrol

14

agents cannot designate ports of entry on a whim. Closing a port of entry temporarily is not

15

synonymous with “de-designating” it as a port of entry. Section 1325 clearly delineates the

16

conduct that it prescribes and is not impermissibly vague.

17
18

Therefore, the Court denies Defendant’s motion to dismiss on the grounds that
§ 1325 is impermissibly vague.

Failure to Allege the Elements of § 1325(a)(1)

20

Defendant argues that the charge should be dismissed because the Government

21

failed to allege that Defendant knew he was an alien when he attempted to enter the United

22

States.

23

The Court disagrees that knowledge of alienage is required under § 1325. Defendant

24

relies in part on Rehaif v. United States, 139 S. Ct. 2191 (2019) to make this argument.

25

Rehaif, however, is distinguishable. In Rehaif, the Supreme Court held that a defendant can

26

be convicted of possession of a firearm by an alien who is illegally or unlawfully present

27

in the United States under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g) only if the Government shows that the

28

defendant knew of his illegal or unlawful immigration status. Rehaif, 139 S. Ct. at 2196.
12
3:20-mj-20066-AHG

 Case 3:20-mj-20066-AHG Document 35 Filed 08/05/20 PageID.234 Page 13 of 16

1

The Court’s interpretation of § 922(g) was governed by the overall statutory scheme, which

2

includes 18 U.S.C. § 924(a)(2). Section 924(a)(2) expressly provides for punishment of a

3

defendant who “knowingly violates” § 922(g). See Rehaif, 139 S. Ct. at 2194. There is no

4

similar express requirement in § 1325, however, that a violation be “knowing.”

5

Rehaif does address the general standard for interpreting the mens rea requirement

6

for an element of a criminal statute. Id. at 2195. The “longstanding presumption” is that

7

“Congress intends to require a defendant to possess a culpable mental state regarding ‘each

8

of the statutory elements that criminalize otherwise innocent conduct.’” Id. (quoting United

9

States v. X-Citement Video, Inc., 513 U.S. 64, 72 (1994)). However, unlike the statute at

10

issue in Rehaif, the element of alienage in a § 1325 charge does not criminalize “otherwise

11

innocent conduct.” See Nunez-Soberanis, 406 F. Supp. 3d at 843-45. The conduct that

12

§ 1325 criminalizes is attempting to cross the border outside the port of entry, free from

13

official restraint. See, e.g., United States v. Argueta-Rosales, 819 F.3d 1149, 1151 (9th Cir.

14

2016); United States v. Lombero-Valdovinos, 429 F.3d 927, 928-29 (9th Cir. 2005). Any

15

person, whether a United States citizen or an alien, who engages in such conduct violates

16

the laws of the United States. Nunez-Soberanis, 406 F. Supp. 3d. at 844 (citing

17

19 U.S.C. § 1459(a)). Therefore, the Government does not need to prove that a defendant

18

knew he or she was an alien to obtain a conviction for violating § 1325(a)(1).

19

Defendant also argues that a defendant who believes he or she is a United States

20

citizen at the time of the alleged offense cannot be found guilty of a violation of

21

§ 1325(a)(1) because “‘a defendant should be treated in accordance with the facts as he

22

supposed them to be,’ not as they actually are.” ECF No. 20-1 at 25 (quoting United States

23

v. Quijada, 588 F.2d 1253, 1255 (9th Cir. 1978)). Quijada does not stretch as far as

24

Defendant would like. The issue in Quijada was whether the defendant, who had the intent

25

to distribute cocaine, could avoid conviction because the substance he distributed was not

26

in fact cocaine. Id. at 1255. A defendant can be convicted of attempted illegal entry under

27

§ 1325 if he or she has the specific intent to enter the country free from official restraint.

28

Lombera-Valdovinos, 429 F.3d at 929. Quijada cannot plausibly be read to hold that a
13

1

defendant could avoid conviction under § 1325 when he or she had the specific intent to

2

enter the country free from official restraint, merely because the defendant had a mistaken

3

belief that he or she was not an alien at the time of the offense.

4
5
6
7

Therefore, the Court denies Defendant’s motion to dismiss on the grounds that the
Government failed to allege all elements of § 1325.

Violation of the Equal Protection Clause under Sessions v.
Morales-Santana

8

Defendant argues that § 1325 is unconstitutional following the U.S. Supreme

9

Court’s opinion in Sessions v. Morales-Santana, 137 S. Ct. 1678, 1686, 1700-01 (2017).

10

In Morales-Santana, the Supreme Court held that the gender-based distinctions for

11

derivative citizenship in 8 U.S.C. §§ 1401(a)(7) and 1409 (a) and (c) are unconstitutional

12

because they violate the Equal Protection Clause. Morales-Santana, 137 S. Ct. at 1698,

13

1700-01. In United States v. Madero-Diaz, 752 F. App’x 537 (9th Cir. 2019), the Ninth

14

Circuit declined to extend Morales-Santana to the definition of “alien” that applies to §

15

1325. Id. at 538. This Court does not have the authority or inclination to overrule the Ninth

16

Circuit Court of Appeals on this issue.

17
18

Therefore, the Court denies Defendant’s motion to dismiss based on the alleged
unconstitutionality of § 1325 under Sessions v. Morales-Santana.
