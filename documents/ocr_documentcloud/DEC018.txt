II.

Legal Standard

2

A. Miranda

3

Before proceeding with a custodial interrogation, a suspect must be advised of his

4

Miranda rights: that he “‘has the right to remain silent, that anything he says can be used

5

against him in a court of law, that he has the right to the presence of an attorney, and that

6

if he cannot afford an attorney one will be appointed for him prior to any questioning if

7

he so desires.’” Dickerson v. United States, 530 U.S. 428, 435 (2000) (quoting Miranda

8

v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 436, 479 (1966)). The police are required to explain these rights to a

9

suspect “before questioning begins,” Davis v. United States, 512 U.S. 452, 457 (1994),

10

and, if a defendant requests counsel, “the interrogation must cease until an attorney is

11

present.” Miranda v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 436, 474 (1966). Statements obtained in violation

12

of Miranda generally are inadmissible in the Government’s case-in-chief. New York v.

13

Harris, 495 U.S. 14, 20 (1990).

14

A suspect’s waiver of these rights is valid only if it is “voluntary, knowing and

15

intelligent.” Miranda, 384 U.S. at 479. Thus, the waiver inquiry “has two distinct

16

dimensions.” Berghuis v. Thompkins, 560 U.S. 370, 382–83 (2010) (quotation omitted).

17

First, it must be “voluntary in the sense that it was the product of a free and deliberate

18

choice rather than intimidation, coercion, or deception.” Id. Second, it must be “made

19

with a full awareness of both the nature of the right being abandoned and the

20

consequences of the decision to abandon it.” Id.

21

A waiver satisfies this two-part standard only “if the totality of the circumstances

22

surrounding the interrogation reveal both an uncoerced choice and the requisite level of

23

comprehension.” Moran v. Burbine, 475 U.S. 412, 421 (1986) (quotation omitted).

24

Among other circumstances, a court may consider “(i) the defendant’s mental capacity;

25

(ii) whether the defendant signed a written waiver; (iii) whether the defendant was

26

advised in his native tongue or had a translator; (iv) whether the defendant appeared to

27

understand his rights; (v) whether the defendant’s rights were individually and repeatedly

28

8

3

States v. Crews, 502 F.3d 1130, 1140 (9th Cir. 2007)).

B. 18 U.S.C. § 3501(c)

5

The right to prompt presentment is found in Rule 5(a), which provides that “[a]

6

person making an arrest within the United States must take the defendant without

7

unnecessary delay before a magistrate judge.” FED. R. CRIM P. 5(a)(1)(A). 18 U.S.C. §

8

3501(c), in turn, governs whether a defendant’s confession may be admissible where the

9

Government violates Rule 5(a). 5 Section 3501(c) codifies the McNabb–Mallory Rule,

10

which rendered a confession inadmissible if it was obtained during an unreasonably long

11

period of detention between a defendant’s arrest and initial court appearance. See

12

generally Mallory v. United States, 354 U.S. 449, 450–51 (1957) (confession obtained

13

around 9:30 p.m. following defendant’s arrest “between two and two-thirty p.m.”);

14

McNabb v. United States, 318 U.S. 332 (1943).

15

A district court hearing a presentment challenge must engage in a two-step inquiry

16

under to § 3501(c). First, the court “must find whether the defendant confessed within six

17

hours of arrest.” Corley v. United States, 556 U.S. 315, 320 (2009) (quotations omitted).

18

“If the confession came within [the safe harbor] period, it is admissible, subject to the

19

(D. Haw. 1995). Section 3501(e) may also apply to statements are, in some sense,
