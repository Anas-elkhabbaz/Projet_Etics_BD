1.

Violation of the Non-Delegation Doctrine

3

Defendant argues that the § 1325 charge against him should be dismissed under the

4

non-delegation doctrine because Congress delegated its core legislative functions to the

5

Executive Branch by allowing immigration officers to determine the times and places when

6

an alien can lawfully enter the United States. See 8 U.S.C. § 1325(a) (defining as one of

7

three alternative elements of the offense the entry or attempted entry into the United States

8

“at any time or place other than as designated by immigration officers”). The non-

9

delegation doctrine generally forbids Congress from transferring “powers which are strictly

10

and exclusively legislative” to other branches of government. Gundy v. United States, 139

11

S. Ct. 2116, 2123 (2019) (citation omitted). Defendant claims that the statute does not

12

provide any intelligible principle to guide this discretion.

13

“In our increasingly complex society, replete with ever changing and more technical

14

problems, [the Supreme] Court has understood that Congress simply cannot do its job

15

absent an ability to delegate power under broad general directives.” Id. (quoting Mistretta

16

v. United States, 488 U.S. 361, 372 (1989)) (internal alteration and quotations omitted).

17

Therefore, as long as “Congress has supplied an intelligible principle to guide the delegee’s

18

use of discretion[,]” a statute does not run afoul of the non-delegation doctrine. In

19

performing the analysis of whether such an “intelligible principle” is supplied, the Court

20

must “constru[e] the challenged statute to figure out what task it delegates and what

21

instructions it provides.” Gundy, 139 S. Ct. at 2123. Importantly, that task of construction

22

does not require the Court to look only at the text of the statute “in isolation.” Rather, the

23

intelligibility of the standards that guide the delegee’s discretion must be considered in

24

light of “the purpose of the [statute at issue], its factual background, and the statutory

25

context in which they appear.” Am. Power & Light Co. v. SEC, 329 U.S. 90, 104 (1946).

26

Construing § 1325 in United States v. Nunez-Soberanis, 406 F. Supp. 3d 835 (S.D.

27

Cal. 2019), another court in this District rejected the same non-delegation argument

28

Defendant presents here, reasoning as follows:

Defendant attempts to read into the statute a broader delegation than
actually occurred by arguing that any individual immigration official can
designate any piece of land as a place for entry. Not so. Congress requires
that aliens seeking lawful entrance to the United States do so at a port of
entry. See United States v. Corrales-Vasquez, 931 F.3d 944, 946 (9th Cir.
2019); United States v. Aldana, 878 F.3d 877, 882 (9th Cir. 2017). Ports
of entry can only be designated or de-designated by the Secretary of
Homeland Security subject to the Administrative Procedures Act. See 8
CFR § 100.4(a). Ports of entry also necessarily include facilities, staffed
by immigration officials that are set up to accept applications for
admission. Aldana, 878 F.3d at 882. To interpret Section 1325(a) to permit
a border patrol agent to designate a portion of the border fence “on a whim”
is in direct conflict with Congress’s clear statutory scheme.
Id. at 839-40. This Court agrees. Contrary to Defendant’s argument, when viewed in the
proper context rather than viewing the statutory text in isolation, § 1325’s delegation to
immigration officers to designate time and place of lawful entry is not unfettered, but
significantly constrained. Am. Power, 329 U.S. at 104; see also Gundy, 139 S. Ct. at 212324 (considering the text of a statute “alongside its context, purpose, and history” to
determine the extent of discretion afforded to the delegee in question).
Congress’s delegation of the duty to designate the time and place for lawful entry of
aliens also does not expand the scope of criminal liability under § 1325. Opening or closing
ports of entry does not change or expand the conduct that leads to criminal liability under
§ 1325. Closing ports or restricting hours of operation may make it more cumbersome for
aliens to enter legally, but it does not create new or different criminal liability.
Therefore, the Court denies Defendant’s motion to dismiss based on the nondelegation doctrine.

5.

Failure to Allege All Elements of Section 1325

2

Defendant argues that the charge should be dismissed because the Government

3

failed to allege that Defendant knew he was an alien when he attempted to enter the United

4

States, and thus failed to allege “all the elements of the charged offense” by omitting a

5

mens rea element in the charging document. ECF No. 26-1 at 4.

6

The Court disagrees that knowledge of alienage is an element of a charge under

7

§ 1325. Defendant relies in part on Rehaif v. United States, 139 S. Ct. 2191 (2019) to make

8

this argument. Rehaif, however, is distinguishable. In Rehaif, the Supreme Court held that

9

a defendant can be convicted of possession of a firearm by an alien who is illegally or

10

unlawfully present in the United States under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g) only if the Government

11

shows that the defendant knew of his illegal or unlawful immigration status. Rehaif, 139 S.

12

Ct. 2196. The Court’s interpretation of § 922(g) was governed by the overall statutory

13

scheme, which includes 18 U.S.C. § 924(a)(2). Section 924(a)(2) expressly provides for

14

punishment of a defendant who “knowingly violates” § 922(g). There is no similar express

15

requirement in 8 U.S.C. § 1325, however, that a violation be “knowing.”

16

Rehaif does address the general standard for interpreting the mens rea requirement

17

for an element of a criminal statute. Id. at 2195. The “longstanding presumption” is that

18

“Congress intends to require a defendant to possess a culpable mental state regarding ‘each

19

of the statutory elements that criminalize otherwise innocent conduct.’” Id. (quoting United

20

States v. X-Citement Video, Inc., 513 U.S. 64, 74 (1994)). However, unlike the statute at

21

issue in Rehaif, the element of alienage in a § 1325 charge does not criminalize “otherwise

22

innocent conduct.” See Nunez-Soberanis, 406 F. Supp. 3d at 843-45. The conduct that

23

§ 1325 criminalizes is attempting to cross the border outside the port of entry, free from

24

official restraint. See, e.g., United States v. Argueta-Rosales, 819 F.3d 1149, 1151 (9th Cir.

25

2016); United States v. Lombero-Valdovinos, 429 F.3d 927, 929 (9th Cir. 2005). Any

26

person, whether a United States citizen or an alien, who engages in such conduct violates

27

the laws of the United States. Nunez-Soberanis, 406 F. Supp. 3d. at 844 (citing

28

19 U.S.C. § 1459(a)). Therefore, the Government does not need to prove that a defendant
11
3:19-mj-24175-AHG

 Case 3:19-mj-24175-AHG-BAS Document 37 Filed 02/13/20 PageID.245 Page 12 of 13

1

knew he or she was an alien to obtain a conviction for violating 8 U.S.C. § 1325(a)(1).

2

Defendant also argues that knowledge of alienage must be alleged because

3

§ 1325(a)(1) charges an attempt crime. But a defendant cannot be convicted of attempted

4

illegal entry under § 1325 unless he or she has the specific intent to enter the country free

5

from official restraint. Lombera-Valdovinos, 429 F.3d at 929. That specific intent is

6

sufficient to meet the mens rea requirement for an attempt crime. See, e.g., United States

7

v. Gracidas-Ulibarry, 231 F.3d 1188, 1193 (9th Cir. 2000) (requiring specific intent for an

8

attempt crime resolves “the uncertainty whether the defendant’s purpose was indeed to

9

engage in criminal, rather than innocent, conduct.”).

10
11
12
13

The Court denies Defendant’s motion to dismiss based on failure to allege all
elements of § 1325.
