The matter before the Court is the motion to suppress statements filed by

16 Defendant. (ECF No. 21-2).
17

On September 24, 2018, Defendant drove a vehicle into the Otay Mesa,

18 California Port of Entry. A Customs and Border Patrol Officer checked the rear cargo
19 area of the vehicle and found a person hidden in the rear passenger-side quarter panel.
20 Defendant was arrested and escorted to the secondary inspection area. Defendant was
21 interviewed by two Customs and Border Patrol Officers. The interview was recorded
22 in its entirety. The video of the interview has been received into evidence.
23

Defendant moves the Court to suppress the statements made during the interview

24 on the grounds that the Government must demonstrate compliance with Miranda, and
25 that his statements were involuntary. Plaintiff United States asserts that the video
26 demonstrates conclusively that the Defendant received proper Miranda warnings, and
27 made a voluntary, intelligent, and knowing decision to waive his rights, and make a
28 statement.

RULING OF THE COURT
“For inculpatory statements made by a defendant during custodial interrogation

3 to be admissible in evidence, the defendant’s waiver of Miranda rights must be
4 voluntary, knowing, and intelligent.” United States v. Garibay, 143 F.3d 534, 536 (9th
5 Cir. 1998)(internal quotation omitted). “Only if the totality of the circumstances
6 surrounding the interrogation reveal both an uncoerced choice and the requisite level
7 of comprehension may a court properly conclude that the Miranda rights have been
8 waived. ” Moran v. Burdine, 475 U.S. 412, 421 (1986) (internal quotation omitted).
9

There is a presumption against waiver which the Government bears the burden

10 of overcoming by a preponderance of evidence. Garibay, 143 F.3d at 536. See
11 Berghuis v. Thompkins, 560 U.S. 370, 384 (2012) (“[T]his ‘heavy burden’ is not more
12 than the burden to establish waiver by a preponderance of the evidence.”) (internal
13 quotation omitted). The validity of a waiver depends upon the particular facts and
14 circumstances of the case, including the background, experience and conduct of the
15 defendant. See Edwards v. Arizona, 451 U.S. 477, 482 (1981). “Several factors to
16 consider are (i) defendant’s mental capacity; (ii) whether the defendant signed a written
17 waiver; (iii) whether the defendant was advised in his native tongue or had a translator;
18 (iv) whether the defendant appeared to understand his rights; (v) whether the
19 defendant’s rights were individually and repeatedly explained to him; and (vi) whether
20 the defendant had prior experience with the criminal justice system.” United States v.
21 Crews, 502 F.3d 1130, 1140 (9th Cir. 2007).
22

The video shows clearly that the officers informed the Defendant of his Miranda

23 rights and that the Defendant acknowledged that he understood each right. The video
24 shows clearly that the Defendant waived his rights orally and in writing. The video
25 shows clearly that the officer asked the Defendant if he wanted to make a statement and
26 the Defendant responded that he wanted to make a statement. Defendant answered each
27 question asked and did not indicate any intention to terminate the interview or to seek
28 the advice of counsel at any point in the interview. Based upon this record, considering

2 its burden to establish by a preponderance of the evidence that Defendant was fully
3 informed of his Miranda rights; that the Defendant acknowledged that he understood
4 his rights; and that the Defendant voluntarily, knowingly, and intelligently waived his
5 rights prior to questioning.
6

“In evaluating voluntariness, the test is whether, considering the totality of the

7 circumstances, the government obtained the statement by physical or psychological
8 coercion or by improper inducement so that the suspect’s will was overborne.” United
9 States v. Male Juvenile, 280 F.3d 1008, 1022 (9th Cir. 2002)(internal quotations
10 omitted). In this case, the video conclusively shows the demeanor of the officers and
11 Defendant remained calm throughout the interview. There is no evidence that voices
12 were raised, that the officers pressured the Defendant, or that threats or false promises
13 of any kind were made by the agents. The video clearly shows Defendant voluntarily
14 answered the questions of the officers with no intimidation or coercion. There are no
15 facts before this Court to support the conclusion that the Defendant’s will was
16 overborne by intimidation, coercion, or deception. Based upon this record, considering
17 the totality of the circumstances, the Court concludes that the Government has carried
18 its burden to establish by a preponderance of the evidence that Defendant’s statements
19 were the voluntary.
20

IT IS HEREBY ORDERED that the motion to suppress statements filed by

21 Defendant (ECF No. 21-2) is denied.
