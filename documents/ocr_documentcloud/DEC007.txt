I.

FACTUAL BACKGROUND

On March 17, 2018, at approximately 1:55 am, Defendant applied for entry into
the United States at the Calexico West Port of Entry. She was driving her white Nissan

4 Versa. A Human and Narcotics Detector dog alerted to the doors of the vehicle, after
5 which a Customs and Border Patrol (“CBP”) officer pried open the rear driver side door
6 panel and observed packages taped to the walls. In secondary inspection, agents
7 discovered 50 packages of a white crystalline substance taped to the inside of all four
8 door panels. The contents of the packages field-tested positive for methamphetamine and
9 weighed approximately 24.6 kilograms. Defendant was arrested.
10

Following her arrest, Defendant was placed in a holding cell at the Port of Entry.

11 Homeland Security Investigations (“HSI”) Special Agent Jonathon Creiglow went to
12 Defendant’s holding cell and asked her in Spanish for some biographical information and
13 whether she had a cellphone or money with her. (Dec. Agent Creiglow, Government’s
14 Opposition, Ex. 1 [ECF NO. 25-1]) There is no video or transcript of the interview.
15 Agent Creiglow did not provide Defendant with Miranda warnings before these
16 questions. (Id.) According to Agent Creiglow, Defendant answered the biographical
17 questions and told Agent Creiglow that the CBP officers had her cell phone and she did
18 not have any money. (Id.)
19

Once the booking paperwork was completed, Agent Creiglow transferred

20 Defendant from the holding cell to an interview room. At approximately 7:18 am, HSI
21 special agents Greiglow and Marroquin interviewed Defendant. The interview was
22 conducted in the Spanish language. The officers read Defendant her Miranda rights from
23 a pre-printed card. Defendant said she wasn’t sure about answering any questions without
24 an attorney. The interviewing agent stated that the decision was hers, and that this was an
25 opportunity for her to tell her side of the story. She agreed speak to the agent and signed
26 the Miranda form. During the ensuing interrogation, she said she would like to speak to
27 an attorney, but after the agent again said this would be her only chance to speak with
28 him, she continued to talk with him.

II.

PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

As to voluntariness, it is the Government’s burden to prove by a preponderance of

13

14 the evidence that a criminal defendant’s statement was voluntary. United States v. Tingle,
15 658 F.2d 1332, 1335 (9th Cir. 1981). Under the Fifth Amendment, a suspect has the right
16 to remain silent and choose to speak only of his own free will. Malloy v. Hogan. 378 U.S.
17 1, 8 (1964). A statement made as a result of physical intimidation or psychological pressure
18 is considered involuntary. Townsend v. Sain, 372 U.S. 293 (1963). Subtle psychological
19 pressure executed by law enforcement suffices, and at times more effectively, to render a
20 confession involuntary. Tingle, 658 F.2d at 1335. The Court must conduct a factual
21 inquiry to determine whether a statement is involuntary, focusing on law enforcement’s
22 conduct in creating pressure, and the defendant’s ability to resist that pressure. Mincey v.
23 Arizona, 437 U.S. 385, 399 (1978). “The due process test takes into consideration the
24 totality of all the surrounding circumstances—both the characteristics of the accused and
25 the details of the interrogation.” Dickerson v. United States, 530 U.S. 428, 434, (2000).
26 These details include “duration and conditions of detention . . . the manifest attitude of the

The Court has viewed the video recording of Defendant’s post-arrest interrogation

5 and after weighing the details of the interrogation, finds that Defendant’s statements were
6 voluntary. Agent Creiglow conducted the interview in a calm manner, asking questions
7 and explaining the process in a normal tone. Defendant was Mirandized. Defendant’s
8 answers to the agent’s questions were often nonresponsive, however the agent did not
9 raise his voice but instead calmly repeated the question and why it was important for her
10 to be honest with him. Neither agent unholstered his weapon or made any express or
11 implied threats to Defendant. Agent Creiglow explained to Defendant that it would be
12 beneficial to her to be honest and forthright immediately, stating more than once that he
13 believed she was not the most culpable person involved in the crime.
14

Defendant is correct that she was held for five hours before the interrogation

15 began, however weighing this factor against the overall tone and conditions of her
16 interrogation, this delay did not appear to weaken Defendant’s ability to assert her rights
17 as her repeated requests for counsel demonstrates, nor answer questions or understand the
18 proceedings. In short, there was no evidence of coercion by either psychological or
19 physical means. The totality of the circumstances do not support Defendant’s contention
20 that her statements were involuntary. Dickerson, 530 U.S. at 434. In light of the above,
21 the Court DENIES Defendant’s Motion to Suppress Statements.

1. Border search exception

23

The government must generally demonstrate probable cause to obtain a warrant to

24 search the property of a suspect, and the search must be reasonable. Riley v. California,
25 134 S.Ct 2473, 2482 (2014). A recognized exception exists for border searches, where
26 there is a recognized right of the sovereign to control who and what enters the country. US
27 v Flores-Montano, 541 US 149, 152-53 (2004)(“The Government's interest in preventing
28 the entry of unwanted persons and effects is at its zenith at the international border.”)

C. Supplemental Motion to Suppress Holding Cell Statements

4

Miranda warnings are not required for routine booking questions, including

5 biographical information necessary for booking. See Pennsylvania v. Muniz, 496 U.S. 582,
6 601 (1990). Routine booking questions do not include questions that are designed to elicit
7 incriminatory admissions. Id. at 602, n 14. The purpose of routine booking questions is to
8 “secure biographical date necessary to complete booking or pretrial services.” United
9 States v. Williams, 842 F.3d 1143, 1153 (9th Cir 2016).
10

Defendant argues that her statements to Agent Creiglow during her holding cell

11 interview must be suppressed because he did not Mirandize her prior to speaking with her,
12 and the questions do not fall within the routine booking questions exception. (Supp. Mot.
13 2) Specifically, Defendant seeks to suppress her statement denying that she had any money
14 in her possession arguing that it was made involuntarily. (Id. at 4). She cites to the five
15 hour wait before questioning began, and that she did not know she could refuse to answer
16 the agent’s questions because she had not received her Miranda warnings. (Id. at 4).
17

In response, the Government argues that the questions Agent Creiglow asked

18 Defendant, including whether she had any money on her, are routine booking questions
19 that do not require Miranda warnings. (Oppo. 6).
20

For the following reasons, the Court finds that the question regarding money was

21 not a routine booking question. Agent Creiglow’s question to Defendant about money in
22 her possession was clearly not designed to elicit biographical information such as her name
23 or birthdate. The Government asserts that the question concerning money in Defendant’s
24 possession is a routine question because “law enforcement officers cannot allow cash into
25 a jail.” (Supp. Mot. Oppo. 6). However, the arresting officers could have conducted an
26 inventory search before Defendant was incarcerated without anyone asking her if she had
27 money on her person. Illinois v. Lafayette, 462 U.S. 640, 643 (1983) (An inventory search
28 of a person under lawful arrest is an incidental administrative step following arrest and
9

17-cv-01901-L

1 preceding incarceration.) Moreover, Officer Creiglow did not need to ask the question
2 before booking her into jail because jail officials were searching individuals as they were
3 being processed for personal items such as money, at which time they could have
4 discovered the cash.
5

To determine whether Agent Creiglow’s question was likely to elicit an

6 incriminating response, the Court looks to the circumstance surrounding her arrest. See
7 United States v. Solano-Godines, 120 F.3d 957, 961 (9th Cir 1997). Defendant was arrested
8 in a car with methamphetamine hidden within the doors, and Agent Creiglow
9 acknowledged in his report that he thoroughly searched Defendant’s “vehicle, personal
10 items and wallet looking for evidence of drug smuggling payment.” (Supp. Mot Ex A,
11 Report). Although asking an arrestee if they have money on their person seems innocuous
12 at first blush, but taken in conjunction with the circumstances surrounding Defendant’s
13 arrest, the question could likely have elicited an incriminating response. For the above
14 reasons, the Court GRANTS Defendant’s Supplemental Motion to Suppress her holding
15 cell statement that she had no money on her person.
