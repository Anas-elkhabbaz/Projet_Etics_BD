B. Analysis

13

Defendant argues that the Court should suppress the evidence found in (1) Wasef’s

14

shirt pocket and (2) the black bag.12 Wasef contends that the searches violated his Fourth

15

Amendment rights and that the statements are fruits of those invalid searches. There is

16

no dispute that the searches of Defendant’s shirt pocket and the black bag were done

17

without a warrant.

18

The government responds that this evidence should not be suppressed because

19

(1) Defendant lacks standing to challenge the search of the black bag, (2) the searches

20

were supported by probable cause, (3) the evidence found in Defendant’s shirt pocket

21

was found as the result of a frisk supported by reasonable suspicion, and (4) the evidence

22

would have been inevitably discovered during inventory searches of Wasef and the

23

vehicle. For the reasons set forth below, the Court concludes that the government has

26

12

occurred. Wasef’s statements offered at trial are relevant only to the issue of his possession of the black

proven by a preponderance of the evidence that officers would have inevitably discovered

2

the evidence found in Wasef’s shirt pocket and the black bag pursuant to inventory

3

searches. As a result, the Court need not address the government’s other arguments.

4

“The inevitable discovery doctrine is an exception to the exclusionary rule. The

5

doctrine permits the government to rely on evidence that ultimately would have been

6

discovered absent a constitutional violation.” United States v. Ruckes, 586 F.3d 713, 718

7

(9th Cir. 2009) (internal quotation marks omitted). “[I]f the government can establish by

8

a preponderance of the evidence that unlawfully obtained [evidence] ‘ultimately or

9

inevitably would have been discovered by lawful means,’ the exclusionary rule will not”

10

preclude the admission of evidence obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment.

11

United States v. Camou, 773 F.3d 932, 943 (9th Cir. 2014) (quoting Nix v. Williams, 467

12

U.S. 431, 444 (1984)). This doctrine reasons that exclusion of such evidence would “put

13

the government in a worse position” than had the misconduct never occurred and would

14

not properly serve any effort at deterrence. Nix, 467 U.S. at 444. The burden on the

15

government, however, is difficult to meet. The inevitable discovery doctrine “requires

16

that ‘the fact or likelihood that makes the discovery inevitable arise from circumstances

17

other than those disclosed by the illegal search itself.’” United States v. Ramirez-

18

Sandoval, 872 F.2d 1392, 1396 (9th Cir. 1989) (quoting United States v. Boatwright, 822

19

F.2d 862, 864–65 (9th Cir. 1987)). Moreover, the Court’s analysis must “involve[] no

20

speculative elements, but [instead] focus[] on demonstrated historical facts capable of

21

ready verification or impeachment.” Nix, 467 U.S. at 444 n.5. Here, the government

22

argues that officers would have inventoried all items found on Wasef’s person and in the

23

vehicle once they had probable cause to believe that the vehicle was stolen.

24

After arresting an individual, officers may lawfully search the arrestee’s person

25

and inventory all items found without first obtaining a warrant to do so. See Illinois v.

26

Lafayette, 462 U.S. 640, 648 (1983) (“[I]t is not ‘unreasonable’ [under the Fourth

27

Amendment] for police, as part of the routine procedure incident to incarcerating an

28

arrested person, to search any container or article in his possession, in accordance with
11
3:18-cr-01483-GPC

 Case 3:18-cr-01483-GPC Document 22 Filed 07/19/18 PageID.65 Page 12 of 28

1

established inventory procedures.”); Ruckes, 586 F.3d at 717 (the search-incident-to-

2

arrest doctrine “permits officers to search an arrestee’s person subsequent to the arrest of

3

that individual”). To demonstrate that the officers would have found the evidence in

4

Defendant’s shirt pocket, the government must prove by a preponderance of the evidence

5

that they (1) would have lawfully arrested Wasef, and (2) would have subsequently

6

searched and inventoried Wasef’s person. The government met its burden on both issues.

7

First, Seddon and Howard testified that when they come upon a potentially stolen

8

vehicle, they detain the occupants until they can determine the status of the vehicle. If

9

the vehicle is reported as stolen, the officers testified, they arrest the occupants. Here,

10

regardless of whether Defendant was arrested—as opposed to merely detained—prior to

11

the officers receiving Saadedin’s report that the vehicle was stolen, Seddon and Howard’s

12

testimony demonstrate that they would not have allowed Wasef to leave prior to

13

receiving that report, and that they would have arrested Wasef after receiving that report.

14

Such an arrest would have been lawful. Rohde v. City of Roseburg, 137 F.3d 1142,

15

1144–45 (9th Cir. 1998) (holding that an officer has probable cause to arrest the driver of

16

a vehicle that has been reported stolen unless the driver offers “proof of ownership that

17

would rebut the evidence that the vehicle had been stolen”). Second, Seddon testified

18

that departmental policy calls for a personal inventory search of every arrestee. Nothing

19

presented by either party suggested that Seddon would not have had Wasef’s belonging

20

inventoried after his arrest. In light of this evidence, the government has proven by a

21

preponderance of the evidence that once the officers received Saadedin’s report that his

22

vehicle was stolen, they would have lawfully arrested and searched Wasef, and would

23

have found the evidence in his shirt pocket.

24

The officers also would have inevitably discovered the contents of the black bag in

25
